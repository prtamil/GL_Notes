
## **How We Work**

Human beings operate through two intertwined layers of functioning—one automatic, the other deliberate. This dual structure shows up in both the body and the mind.

In the body, many essential processes run without conscious effort. The heart beats, lungs breathe, digestion unfolds—these are automatic systems that sustain life. Alongside them are voluntary actions: we choose to move our hands, speak, walk, or build. Life works because of this balance between what happens _to us_ and what we _choose to do_.

The mind follows a similar pattern.

### **The Automatic Mind: The Stream We Don’t Control**

Thoughts often arise uninvited—random, emotional, sometimes irrational. They can be kind, disturbing, creative, or meaningless. This stream is not something we consciously design; it simply appears.

Modern psychology, especially through thinkers like Daniel Kahneman, describes this as fast, automatic thinking—what he calls _System 1_ in Cognitive Psychology. It is reactive, associative, and constantly active.

In spiritual framing, this automatic flow can be seen as part of human nature—something given, not chosen. Because of that, holding guilt or fear over every passing thought is misguided. A thought is not an action. It is just a signal passing through.

This is where many people struggle: they mistake _having a thought_ for _being responsible for it_. But the automatic mind is not the moral center—it is just the generator.

### **The Deliberate Mind: Where Responsibility Begins**

The second layer begins when we engage with those thoughts.

We can:

- Accept or reject them
    
- Act on them or let them pass
    
- Build on them or ignore them
    

This is the manual system—the part where intention, judgment, and character live.

A harmful thought may arise automatically. But choosing to act on it, encourage it, or dwell in it—that is where responsibility begins. Similarly, a good thought that is never acted upon remains only potential.

So morality and accountability do not belong to the _appearance_ of thoughts, but to our _response_ to them.

### **The Role of Guidance**

Since the automatic stream cannot be fully controlled, the question becomes: how do we guide our responses?

Spiritual traditions often suggest prayer or surrender—not as a passive escape, but as a way to align the deliberate mind with something higher than impulse. It’s less about stopping thoughts and more about choosing a direction despite them.

In practical terms, this guidance can also come from:

- Reflection
    
- Discipline
    
- Values
    
- Habits
    

Prayer, in this context, becomes a way of strengthening the _chooser_, not eliminating the _noise_.

### **Writers and Cashers: The Illusion of Intention**

The analogy of “writing cheques vs cashing them” captures this perfectly.

Many people “write cheques” in their minds:

- “I will build something great”
    
- “I will become disciplined”
    
- “I will do meaningful work”
    

These are thoughts, intentions, even dreams—but they exist only in the automatic or imagined layer unless acted upon.

The one who “cashes the cheque” is the person who converts thought into action. They engage the deliberate system. They endure friction, doubt, and effort.

The difference is not in ideas—both people may have the same thoughts. The difference is in execution.

### **Bringing It Together**

So, how do we work?

1. Thoughts arise automatically—we don’t control their arrival.
    
2. We interpret and respond deliberately—this is where control exists.
    
3. Our life is shaped not by what appears in our mind, but by what we choose to act on.
    

A person is not defined by the chaos of their thoughts, but by the pattern of their actions.

Understanding this removes unnecessary guilt while increasing meaningful responsibility. It allows us to say:

- “This thought is not me.”
    
- “But what I do next—that is me.”
    

And over time, the one who consistently chooses action over imagination becomes the one who actually _lives_ rather than merely _intends_.
