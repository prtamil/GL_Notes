# Descriptive Statistics – The Language of Describing a Crowd

Think of every dataset as **a crowd of people**.

The people could represent:

- Students' marks
    
- Heights
    
- Salaries
    
- Temperatures
    
- Sales
    
- Any collection of measurements
    

Descriptive statistics asks simple questions to understand this crowd.

It **does not predict**.

It simply **describes what it sees.**

---

# The Six Questions

```text
Dataset (Crowd)
       │
       ├── Where is the center?
       ├── Who is in the middle?
       ├── What is most common?
       ├── How messy is the crowd?
       ├── How far is a typical person from the center?
       └── What shape does the whole crowd make?
```

---

# 1. Mean → Balance Point ⚖️

Think of a giant seesaw.

Everyone stands according to their value.

```text
Small Values           Large Values

👤 👤 👤 👤 👤 👤 👤

          ▲
    Balance Point
```

The mean is the **balance point** of the entire crowd.

It answers:

> **"Where is the center of balance?"**

It describes the center of the **whole group**, not any one individual.

---

# 2. Median → Middle Person 🚶

Arrange everyone from smallest to largest.

```text
👤 👤 👤 👤 👤 👤 👤
        ↑
      Middle
```

The median is simply the **person standing in the middle**.

It answers:

> **"Who is in the middle?"**

The median is not affected much by extremely small or large values.

It represents the **typical person**.

---

# 3. Mode → Most Common ⭐

Look at what appears most often.

```text
Shoe Sizes

8 8 8 8 9 9 10
```

Mode = **8**

It answers:

> **"What do I see most often?"**

Mode is about **frequency**, not the center.

---

# 4. Variance → Messiness 🌪️

Imagine two classrooms.

```text
Low Variance

🙂🙂🙂🙂🙂🙂🙂
```

Everyone is similar.

---

```text
High Variance

🙂      🙂
    🙂
         🙂
```

Everyone is spread out.

Variance answers:

> **"How messy or scattered is this crowd?"**

- Small variance → Similar observations
    
- Large variance → Very different observations
    

---

# 5. Standard Deviation → Typical Distance 📏

Once we know the center, we ask:

> **"How far is a typical person from the center?"**

```text
Center

     🙂
  🙂🙂🙂🙂🙂
🙂🙂🙂🙂🙂🙂🙂
```

Small standard deviation

→ Most people stay close to the center.

Large standard deviation

→ People are spread farther away.

It tells us how **consistent** or **predictable** the data is.

---

# 6. Distribution → Shape of the Crowd 🏔️

Imagine viewing the crowd from a drone.

```text
One Hill

      ▲
    ▲▲▲▲
  ▲▲▲▲▲▲▲
```

Or

```text
Two Groups

▲▲▲      ▲▲▲
```

Distribution answers:

> **"What shape does the entire crowd make?"**

It describes the **overall pattern** of the dataset.

---

# Summary

|Statistic|Think of it as|Question it answers|
|---|---|---|
|Mean|Balance Point|Where is the center?|
|Median|Middle Person|Who is in the middle?|
|Mode|Most Common|What do I see most often?|
|Variance|Messiness|How scattered is the crowd?|
|Standard Deviation|Typical Distance|How far is a typical person from the center?|
|Distribution|Shape of the Crowd|What shape does the whole crowd make?|

---

# Memory Trick

```text
Crowd
 │
 ├── Center?            → Mean
 ├── Middle Person?     → Median
 ├── Most Common?       → Mode
 ├── Messy?             → Variance
 ├── Typical Distance?  → Standard Deviation
 └── Overall Shape?     → Distribution
```

These six questions are the **foundation of descriptive statistics**.

Don't memorize the names. Imagine looking at a crowd and asking these natural questions. Every statistical term is simply a different way of describing that crowd.
